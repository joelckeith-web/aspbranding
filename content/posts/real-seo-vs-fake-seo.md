There's "SEO" that builds your rankings to last, and "SEO" that Google now treats as spam — and from the outside, the monthly invoice can look identical. The difference shows up at the worst possible moment: a Google update rolls out, and the rankings you've been paying for vanish overnight. This post explains, in plain English, what Google's spam policies actually say, how real SEO differs from the fake kind, and how to audit what your agency is really doing.

If your rankings have ever spiked and then collapsed after a "Google update," you've probably already paid for fake SEO without knowing it. The good news is that the tells are easy to spot once you know what to look for, and the real version — the kind that holds — isn't a secret. It's just harder work, which is exactly why some agencies sell the shortcut instead.

## The bait: cheap SEO that ranks for a minute

The pitch is seductive. An agency promises fast rankings, a rising "Domain Authority" score, and a flood of backlinks, all for a low monthly fee. For a few weeks or months, it might even seem to work. Your rankings tick up. The reports look great.

Then an update hits, and it all unwinds. The rankings drop, the traffic dries up, and the agency explains it away as "just a Google update, everyone got hit." What actually happened is that the lift was built on tactics Google's systems are specifically designed to discount. When the update tightened the screws, the artificial boost evaporated and your rankings fell back to what your real content and authority justify — which, if all the budget went to tricks, isn't much.

**Key Takeaway:** Fast, cheap SEO built on links and tricks can rank for a while, then collapse at the next update. If your rankings have spiked and crashed before, that's the signature of rented rankings.

## What Google's spam policies actually say

You don't have to read the technical docs to understand the rules. Google's [spam policies for Search](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) spell out the practices that can get a site discounted or removed. Here are the ones home service operators actually run into, in plain English.

- **Link spam.** Buying links, trading links, or running schemes to pile up backlinks purely to manipulate rankings. Since Google's 2022 link spam update, its systems largely *neutralize* these links — they pass close to zero value — instead of always penalizing the site. So bought links have mostly become wasted money rather than an instant death sentence, but they're still against the rules.
- **Scaled content abuse.** Mass-producing pages — often AI-spun — just to rank for keywords, with no real value to a reader. Pumping out 200 thin "city + service" pages is the classic version.
- **Doorway pages.** Batches of near-identical pages built only to funnel searchers to one destination, rather than to genuinely serve each search. Thin location-page clones are the home service example.
- **Keyword stuffing.** Cramming the same phrases into copy, headings, and hidden text. It reads badly to humans because it was written for a machine that no longer rewards it.
- **Sneaky redirects and cloaking.** Showing search engines one thing and visitors another. Rare in home services, but a sign of an agency willing to cut corners.

The throughline: Google's whole system is built to reward content made for people and discount content made to game the algorithm. Our own [SEO and AEO approach](/blog/ai-optimization-aeo-home-service-businesses-2025) is built on the same principle, because it's the only one that survives updates.

**Key Takeaway:** Google's spam policies target bought links, mass-produced thin pages, doorway clones, and keyword stuffing. All of them are shortcuts the search systems are specifically built to discount.

## Real SEO vs. fake SEO, side by side

Same goal — higher rankings — opposite methods. Here's the contrast.

- **Earning links vs. buying them.** Real SEO earns editorial links because your content is worth citing. Fake SEO buys or trades links, which now pass near-zero value and break the rules.
- **Useful content vs. mass-produced filler.** Real SEO builds genuinely helpful pages that answer a searcher's question. Fake SEO spins out thin, repetitive pages to chase keywords.
- **One real page per service area vs. doorway clones.** Real SEO writes a distinct, locally relevant page for each city you serve. Fake SEO clones one template across 50 towns.
- **Site health vs. tricks.** Real SEO fixes crawlability, speed, structure, and schema. Fake SEO ignores the foundation and leans on hacks.
- **Real metrics vs. vanity scores.** Real SEO reports on rankings, organic traffic, and leads. Fake SEO sells you a third-party "Domain Authority" number Google doesn't even use.

That last point matters more than operators realize. Domain Authority and similar scores are invented by third-party tools, not by Google. Chasing them is chasing a number that doesn't touch your actual rankings. We dig into the durable version in our [guide to choosing the right SEO agency](/blog/choosing-the-right-seo-agency-tips-for-small-business-success) and our breakdown of [Google Business Profile versus website priority](/blog/gbp-vs-website-home-service-priority).

**Key Takeaway:** Real SEO earns links, builds useful pages, fixes the site, and reports real results. Fake SEO buys links, mass-produces filler, clones pages, and sells vanity scores. The first holds through updates; the second doesn't.

## How to audit what your agency is doing

You can pressure-test your current SEO without being technical. Ask and look for these.

- **Ask where your backlinks come from.** If the answer is "we build hundreds of links a month," ask from where. A pile of links from random directories and foreign blogs is the manipulation footprint Google neutralizes.
- **Look at the content being published.** Read a few pages. Do they sound like they were written for a homeowner, or stuffed for a search engine? Would you hand one to a customer?
- **Check your service-area pages.** Are they distinct and genuinely local, or the same paragraph with the city name swapped? Clones are doorway pages.
- **Ask which metrics they report.** Rankings, organic traffic, calls, and leads are real. "Domain Authority went up" is a deflection.
- **Watch the pattern after updates.** If your rankings reliably tank with each core update, the foundation is rented, not owned.

**Key Takeaway:** Audit your SEO by checking where links come from, whether content reads for humans, whether service-area pages are unique, which metrics get reported, and how your rankings behave after updates.

## Common questions

### How do I know if my SEO is legit?

Ask your agency what they actually do to earn rankings, then listen for the difference between content and manipulation. Real SEO is building genuinely useful pages, a fast and crawlable site, accurate local listings, and earning links because the content is worth citing. Fake SEO is buying links, spinning out mass low-quality pages, and chasing third-party "authority scores" Google doesn't use. If the answer is mostly about volume of links or a Domain Authority number, be skeptical — that's the work most likely to collapse at the next update.

### Are paid backlinks against Google's rules?

Yes. Buying or exchanging links to manipulate rankings is a violation of Google's link spam policies. Since the 2022 link spam update, Google's systems largely neutralize manipulative links — they pass close to zero value — rather than always penalizing the site. So bought links have become mostly a waste of money rather than an instant penalty, but they're still against the rules and still a risk. The links that count are editorial ones earned because your content is worth referencing.

### Why did my rankings disappear after a Google update?

Usually because they were built on tactics Google's updates are designed to discount — bought links, thin or mass-produced content, doorway pages, or keyword-stuffed copy. When an update rolls out, that artificial lift gets removed and rankings snap back to what your real content and authority justify. Rankings built on genuinely useful content and a sound site don't collapse like that; they tend to hold or improve through updates. A sudden drop is a sign the foundation was rented, not owned.

## The takeaway

Real SEO and fake SEO chase the same goal with opposite methods, and the bill looks the same until an update separates them. Real SEO earns links, builds useful content, fixes your site, and reports real results. Fake SEO buys links, mass-produces pages, clones doorways, and hides behind vanity scores — and it falls apart the moment Google tightens the rules. The version that lasts is the one built for your customers, not the algorithm.
