Most marketing agencies show you the highlight reel: a new campaign launch, a chart trending up, a badge from an industry award. What they rarely show you is an ordinary Tuesday. For home service businesses, the ordinary Tuesday is where the real work happens, and it tells you far more about what working with an agency is like than any case study ever will.

Here is what one real week looked like on our side of the desk: the calls, the cleanups, and the small requests that never appear in a case study. Very little of it would show up on a scope of work, and taken together it is what separates an agency that runs your campaigns from one that has a stake in whether your business grows.

## What a Home Service Marketing Agency Does Each Week

An office manager at an HVAC client couldn't get into her business's Google account to respond to customer reviews. We worked the issue with her over email that same afternoon until it was fixed.

That same client had an email account phished the week before. We sent over a lockdown checklist: password reset, active sessions killed, forwarding rules checked, two-factor authentication turned back on. We followed up until every box was closed.

Their careers page delivered seven technician applications to their inbox in four days. Technicians research employers the same way homeowners research contractors, so a weak careers page costs a business candidates long before anyone notices it costing them customers.

A home-inspection client mentioned that work had slowed. The same day, we were reviewing ad spend and mapping which neighboring territories to push into, using the same [local SEO strategies](/blog/local-seo-for-home-service-businesses) that keep inspectors visible in tighter markets.

An HVAC owner got a 12-month financial model from us before we let him commit to next year's marketing budget, so the number came out of his own lead volume and close rate instead of a guess.

A patio builder's site forwarded two new leads in one afternoon. A separate client asked us to remove one person from their email list, and it was done within the hour. A client in a regulated industry had every new page reviewed line by line by their compliance lead before anything went live, which added days to the timeline and kept the pages defensible.

**Key Takeaway:** The real work of home service marketing is a hundred small moving parts, and most of them keep working only because somebody answers the email the same day.

## Why the Boring Stuff Protects Your Business

A locked Google account or a phished inbox rarely gets filed under marketing, but for a local service business the damage lands squarely on the marketing side. Your Google Business Profile is often the first thing a homeowner sees before they call, and if you cannot log in to respond to a review or correct your hours, that visibility works against you instead of for you. This is one reason [Google Business Profile management often matters more than the website itself](/blog/gbp-vs-website-home-service-priority) for local service businesses.

Phishing is not a rare event either. The [FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Report](https://www.ic3.gov/) counted 24,768 business email compromise complaints in a single year, with losses topping $3 billion, an average of about $122,900 per incident. Most of these incidents never make the news. They happen to small operators, like an HVAC office manager whose stolen credentials open the door to a scheduling system, a customer list, or a bank account.

The fix is unglamorous and mechanical: password reset, session review, forwarding rules checked, two-factor turned back on. Run in the first few hours, that checklist is what keeps a compromised inbox from becoming a compromised bank account. Businesses that store customer data and job information in field software should treat [data security as part of daily operations](/blog/field-software-data-security), not a one-time setup task.

**Key Takeaway:** A marketing partner that treats account security and review management as part of the job, not an add-on, prevents small problems from turning into six-figure ones.

## Hiring Is a Marketing Problem Too

A careers page that converts well is a hiring tool with measurable financial upside. [Plumbing & Mechanical](https://www.pmmag.com/) reported that construction job-changers earned an average 12.9% pay bump in June, nearly double the rate seen in other industries, while the median age of electricians and plumbers keeps dropping as veterans retire faster than new technicians enter the trade.

In a labor market where good technicians are weighing three other offers, a weak careers page leaks candidates the same way a broken quote form leaks customers. Businesses that want to [move beyond word-of-mouth for both leads and hiring](/blog/beyond-word-of-mouth-lead-generation) need a careers page built with the same care as their service pages.

**Key Takeaway:** A careers page that gets ignored is a missed opportunity in a labor market where wages are rising fast and skilled workers are scarce.

## Budgets Should Come From the Math

An HVAC owner asked us for help planning next year's marketing spend. Before we let him commit to a number, we built a 12-month financial model based on his lead volume, close rate, and revenue per job. The budget came from that math, not from an industry rule of thumb or a competitor's spend.

This is the same reasoning behind [knowing when to bring in a fractional CFO](/blog/when-to-hire-fractional-cfo-home-service) for a growing home service business. Marketing spend and financial planning are not separate conversations. A business that tracks where its leads come from, using solid [marketing attribution](/blog/marketing-attribution-home-service-businesses), has the data needed to build a budget that matches reality instead of guesswork.

**Key Takeaway:** Any agency willing to build the financial model before asking for the budget is showing you how they will handle your money for the rest of the relationship.

## The Ordinary-Week Test: How to Vet Your Agency

You do not need a case study to judge a marketing agency. You need one honest question: what happened last Tuesday?

- **Ask what happened last Tuesday.** A vendor who can answer with specifics, which account and which hour, is doing the work. One who reaches for a case study is selling instead.

- **Time how fast the small stuff gets handled.** Give them a locked account, a bad review, or an unsubscribe request and watch the clock, because same-day turnaround on those usually predicts how the expensive problems get handled.

- **Ask what's not on the invoice.** A good partner can name the unbilled work without stopping to think: the technician application that came through, the slowdown caught early, the compliance review done properly. If nobody can name a single thing, there probably isn't one.

This same logic applies when [choosing an SEO agency](/blog/choosing-the-right-seo-agency-tips-for-small-business-success) or any marketing partner for a home service business. Ask about response time before you ask about pricing.

**Key Takeaway:** If your current agency only surfaces on invoice day, you are paying for output and getting none of the maintenance.

## Compliance and Trust: The Unsubscribe Rule

The [FTC's CAN-SPAM guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business) gives businesses up to 10 business days to honor an email opt-out request, with violations running up to $53,088 each. Ten business days is the outer limit the law allows. Most businesses that wait that long have already lost the subscriber's goodwill.

When a client asked us to remove someone from their email list, it was done within the hour. The fastest way to lose a subscriber's trust is to keep emailing them after they have asked you to stop, and the clock matters here the same way [speed-to-lead matters when a new customer calls in](/blog/speed-to-lead-home-service-businesses). Response time shapes how people judge a business whether they are arriving or leaving.

**Key Takeaway:** How fast a business honors a small request like an unsubscribe is a reliable preview of how it handles bigger commitments.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does a home service marketing agency actually do day to day?

Beyond running ads and posting content, a good agency handles account security, hiring pages, review management, ad spend reviews, unsubscribe requests, and compliance checks. Most of this work never shows up on an invoice, but it protects your business every week.

### Why is a locked Google Business Profile a big deal?

If an office manager or owner can't log in to respond to reviews or update hours, the business loses control of its most visible search asset. Fast recovery matters because reviews and profile activity affect local search rankings.

### How much does a phishing attack cost a small business?

The FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Report found business email compromise cost victims an average of about $122,900 per incident, based on 24,768 reported complaints and just over $3 billion in total losses.

### Is a careers page really part of marketing?

Yes. Technicians research employers the same way homeowners research contractors. A careers page that converts well can deliver multiple qualified applications in days, which matters as trade wages rise and workers change jobs faster.

### How fast do businesses have to honor an email unsubscribe request?

The FTC's CAN-SPAM guide gives businesses up to 10 business days to honor an opt-out, with violations costing up to $53,088 each. Ten business days is the maximum the law allows, and most businesses that take it lose the subscriber's goodwill in the meantime.

### What is the 'ordinary-week test' for vetting a marketing agency?

Ask what happened last Tuesday, not the highlight reel. Time how fast they handle small problems like a locked account or a bad review. Ask what work they did that never showed up on an invoice. The answers show how a vendor handles the expensive problems.

### Should marketing budgets be set before or after reviewing the numbers?

After. A responsible agency builds a financial model first, so the budget comes from real math on lead volume, close rate, and revenue per job, not a guess or an industry rule of thumb.
