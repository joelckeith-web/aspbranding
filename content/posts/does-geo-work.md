Does generative engine optimization work? Going into 2027, the evidence says it works in a narrow, measurable way, and most of the bigger promises made in its name are still unproven. GEO, short for [generative engine optimization](/blog/what-is-generative-engine-optimization), is the work of getting your business named and described correctly when someone asks an AI assistant like ChatGPT or Perplexity a question. Lab studies show that rewriting a page can change how much an AI answer draws from it. A peer-reviewed benchmark from 2025, however, found that most published rewriting methods were largely ineffective.

A July 2026 review of 45 GEO studies, posted as a preprint and not peer reviewed, concluded that the evidence is narrow. In the author's reading, content an AI has already retrieved can change how it gets cited or used, but no reviewed technique showed a stable causal effect, over time and across platforms, on whether people find a site on their own or on what they do next. We also could not find a published controlled test showing that GEO work made an AI assistant recommend a local business more often.

At ASP, a growth-systems marketing agency for home service operators, we refreshed this post in November 2026 with the newest research and platform guidance. It covers what the studies found, what Google, Microsoft, OpenAI and Perplexity say, what you can now measure and how to judge a GEO pitch. If you are new to the terms, our [comparison of SEO, PPC, AEO and GEO](/blog/seo-vs-ppc-vs-aeo-vs-geo) explains how they fit together.

## What Did the Original GEO Research Find?

The term comes from a research paper, [GEO: Generative Engine Optimization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09735), first posted in November 2023 and accepted to the KDD 2024 conference, so it went through peer review. The authors built a benchmark of 10,000 queries and tested different ways of rewriting the web pages an AI engine used to answer them. They measured how much of each answer came from each page and how early that material appeared. Their headline finding was that GEO can boost visibility in generative engine responses by up to 40%.

The methods that worked best were ordinary editing choices. Citing sources, adding quotations and adding statistics produced a relative improvement of 30 to 40% on the paper's main word-count measure, and 15 to 30% on its subjective impression measure. Keyword stuffing, the old habit of repeating search terms, offered little to no improvement.

Lower-ranked pages gained the most. Citing sources raised visibility 115.1% for pages ranked fifth in Google's results, while the top-ranked page lost 30.3% of its visibility on average. Pages that backed up their claims with sources, numbers and quotes earned more space in the answers. In our view, that points to careful writing more than to a new kind of service.

**Key Takeaway:** The founding GEO study found that sourced, specific writing earned more space in AI answers, while keyword stuffing did almost nothing.

## What Are the Limits of the Original GEO Study?

The paper is careful work, but its setup is a long way from a homeowner asking ChatGPT for a plumber. According to the [paper's full text](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.09735v3), the main test engine fetched only the top five Google results for each query. The answer was then written by the GPT-3.5 Turbo model. A page had to be in those five before any rewriting could help it, so GEO in this experiment worked on top of search rankings.

The authors expected that limit to loosen. They wrote that as longer inputs become affordable, future AI models should be able to take in more sources, which would reduce the impact of search rankings. They also noted that their methods may need to adapt over time as AI engines change, the same way SEO has changed.

The live test had limits too. The researchers ran their methods on Perplexity using a subset of 200 samples. Perplexity does not let users pick source URLs, so they uploaded the source text as files. That shows how the model treated text it was handed, and it does not show how Perplexity finds and chooses pages on the open web.

The paper's visibility measures count words in an answer and score its impression. Neither one tracks phone calls, booked jobs or whether the AI recommended a business by name.

Google's own AI answers also look less like that top-five setup, if one vendor study is right. Ahrefs, a search software company that sells an AI-visibility product, analyzed 863,000 keyword results pages and 4 million AI Overview URLs in a [2026 study](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ai-overview-citations-top-10/). It found Google selecting far fewer AI Overview sources straight from the regular results, about 38% compared with about 76% in its July 2025 study. Ahrefs also says it improved its parsing method between the two studies, so part of that drop may come from the method rather than from Google.

Anyone quoting the 40% figure as a forecast for a local contractor is stretching a lab result past what it measured.

**Key Takeaway:** The founding GEO study tested pages that already ranked in Google's top five, with answers written by GPT-3.5 Turbo, and it counted words rather than calls, so it cannot predict what a GEO project will do for your phones.

## Do GEO Rewriting Methods Still Work in Newer Tests?

Two newer peer-reviewed studies point in different directions. [C-SEO Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11097), accepted at NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks 2025, tested published "conversational SEO" methods, meaning ways of rewriting content so an AI favors it. It covered two tasks, question answering and product recommendation, with three domains each.

The C-SEO Bench authors found that most of these methods were largely ineffective and often hurt a document's ranking, the opposite of what was expected. Traditional SEO strategies, which aim to improve where a source ranks in the material the AI reads, were significantly more effective. The study also tested what happens when more sites use the same methods. As the number of adopters went up, the overall gains went down, which the authors describe as a congested, zero-sum problem. In our reading, a rewriting trick that every competitor in a market copies is unlikely to keep helping anyone.

[AutoGEO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.11438), accepted at ICLR 2026, points the other way. It is a system that learns how to rewrite content. Its authors report that experiments on the standard GEO-Bench benchmark and two new benchmarks built from real user queries showed that AutoGEO was effective at increasing content traction while preserving search utility. Those were lab benchmarks with one site rewriting at a time. The result shows that rewriting can still work in a controlled setup, and it does not show what happens in a live assistant or for a local business.

The July 2026 [review of 45 GEO studies](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.14035) tries to reconcile results like these. It was written by a single author and has not been peer reviewed. It says the original paper's gains hold within its experimental setting but depend on a source already being present in a fixed context. It also reports that topical relevance and context position are the most reproducible levers, that generic rules of thumb transfer poorly, that competition can erode individual gains and that rewrites aimed at earning citations can hurt retrieval. Topical relevance is how closely a page matches the question, and context position is where the page sits in the material the AI reads.

Researchers are also asking for a common way to test GEO. An [October 2026 position paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23541) argues that differences between benchmarks can mix up gains from better optimization methods with gains from having more freedom to edit sources. Until that is settled, one study's percentage is hard to compare with another's.

**Key Takeaway:** Rewriting a page for AI has lab support, but the peer-reviewed C-SEO Bench found most published methods largely ineffective and found the gains shrinking as more competitors used them.

## What Decides Whether ChatGPT Cites Your Page?

The newest research splits AI visibility into steps. The assistant first runs its own search and retrieves pages, and then it decides which of those pages to cite and how much to use them. The evidence from 2026 puts most of the weight on that first step.

An [October 2026 preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15033), written by two of the authors of the 2025 University of Toronto study covered below, studied 158,099 answers with 468,037 citations. The answers came from two 90-day collections of commercial questions in two contrasting markets, across ChatGPT, Gemini and Perplexity. Its central finding is about ChatGPT. The authors found that every page ChatGPT cited had been retrieved by its own search, and that which pages that search retrieves explains far more of the variation than the pages' own properties do, 32% against 2.8%.

ChatGPT's searches are not Google's searches either. The authors found that the model rewrites its search queries on every answer, and those queries rarely match Google's or Bing's results. The model's own knowledge plays a part too. ChatGPT adds brands it already knows to its searches, favors websites it knows and names brands without any page to back them up.

Page content still counted in one way. When the authors compared pages on the same website, pages whose titles and paragraphs matched the questions people ask were cited more. Within a single site, the page that best matched the question did better, while features like FAQ blocks and schema only showed up as differences between whole websites.

A [second October 2026 preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15382) followed four ChatGPT web settings, from ChatGPT 5.2 to 5.5. It found that across repeated runs, candidate sources retrieved more often were also more likely to be cited. Google Search and the OpenAI Responses API, even combined, recovered only about one quarter of the URLs ChatGPT cited. The authors suggest that GEO strategies should be re-evaluated whenever ChatGPT's deployed setting or serving environment changes.

A September 2026 [single-author preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.23162), written by the founder and CEO of the company that makes Aiso, a commercial AI-visibility tool, looked at the same question using data from 75 anonymized Aiso projects. It covered 34,960 unbranded prompt observations on GPT and Gemini from June to September 2026. When neither the brand nor its own website showed up in what the engine retrieved, the brand was mentioned 2.8% of the time on GPT and 3.8% on Gemini. With a citation of the brand's own site but no search that included the brand, those rates rose to 49.0% and 58.4%. With both that citation and what the paper calls a branded fan-out, a search by the engine that included the brand, they reached 91.4% and 100%.

![Bars: brand mention rates were 2.8% GPT and 3.8% Gemini when neither the brand nor its site appeared in retrieval, 49.0% and 58.4% with an own-site citation but no branded search, 91.4% and 100% with both.](/images/blog/does-geo-work-retrieval-mention-rates.svg)
*Observational data from one vendor's tool, not peer reviewed. It shows association, not cause.*

The author says the paper's fitted equation is predictive and observational, not a causal description of how the private engines work inside. The projects came from one vendor's tool, so they are not a random sample of brands.

Two other 2026 preprints add detail. One [analysis of 602 controlled prompts](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25707) across ChatGPT, Google's AI Overviews and Gemini, and Perplexity found that "high-influence pages tend to be longer, more structured, semantically aligned, and richer in extractable evidence such as definitions, numerical facts, comparisons, and procedural steps." That study describes patterns and does not test cause. A [controlled replay study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15164) found that structured formatting concentrated citation credit rather than clearly helping a source get admitted to the answer, and that 15% of citation decisions changed when the same answers were generated again.

For a contractor, our reading is that formatting tweaks come second. The first job is being a page the assistant's search can find, with titles and paragraphs that match the questions homeowners ask. Our guide on [how to rank in AI search](/blog/how-to-rank-in-ai-search) goes further into that work.

**Key Takeaway:** In 2026 research, whether ChatGPT's own search found a page explained far more of its citations than page features did, so being found comes before any formatting change.

## Do AI Tools Cite Third-Party Sites or Your Own Website?

This is where the studies disagree most, and an owner deserves to hear both sides. The 2025 University of Toronto study, [Generative Engine Optimization: How to Dominate AI Search](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.08919), is still a preprint. It found that AI search shows a systematic and overwhelming bias toward earned media, meaning third-party, authoritative sources, over brand-owned and social content. In U.S. automotive searches, AI search delivered 81.9% earned media and 18.1% brand sources. Google's mix for the same searches was 45.1% earned, 39.5% brand and 15.4% social.

Two vendor datasets found close to the opposite. Yext, which sells listings management, analyzed 6.8 million AI citations from ChatGPT, Gemini and Perplexity collected between July 1 and August 31, 2025. The questions covered retail, financial services, healthcare and food service. Yext [reported](https://yext.com/about/news-media/ai-citations-release), "The research shows that 86% of citations come from sources brands already control, such as websites and listings." First-party websites made up 44% of citations, listings 42% and reviews and social 8%.

Ranqo, an AI-visibility vendor, published a [preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.20065) on more than 100,000 prompt responses for over 100 brands it tracked from March to May 2026. When engines cited sources, it found that about 78% of those citations went to corporate websites. It also found a size gap. Household names appeared in 73% of relevant AI answers on their first run, mid-market and regional brands in 44%, and niche and small brands in just 11%. Ranqo also found that whether a brand was framed positively or negatively changed about 6.7 times more often than whether it was mentioned at all.

The three studies asked different questions in different industries. Toronto's citation-mix figures come from automotive searches, Yext used branded and unbranded questions in four industries, and Ranqo tracked brands on its own platform. None of these citation-mix figures come from home service searches, so we would not pick one as the rule.

In our reading, a contractor should work both sides. Keep your own website and listings accurate, because they are sources you control. Earn real mentions on other sites by doing good work, because Google lists pursuing inauthentic mentions among the tactics you can ignore.

**Key Takeaway:** Studies disagree on whether AI tools favor third-party sites or a brand's own website and listings, so keep your own sources accurate and earn real mentions elsewhere.

## Do AI Assistants Pick the Same Local Businesses as Google?

The Toronto study also ran local searches, which is where it comes closest to home service. The researchers sent local service searches to Google and to GPT-4o search preview, the search-enabled version of GPT-4o, and measured the overlap in the website domains each one surfaced. Home cleaning had the highest overlap at 20.6%, followed by roofing at 17.1%, tax preparation at 15.4% and dentists at 11.9%. Auto repair came in at 2.5% and IT support at 0.1%.

![Bar chart: overlap between Google and GPT-4o search preview for local searches was 20.6% home cleaning, 17.1% roofing, 15.4% tax prep, 11.9% dentists, 2.5% auto repair, 0.1% IT support.](/images/blog/does-geo-work-local-overlap.svg)
*Share of website domains that both Google and GPT-4o search preview surfaced for local service searches, by category. Source: University of Toronto, 2025, arXiv preprint 2509.08919 (not peer reviewed).*

The authors read this as a sign that AI engines line up less with Google when surfacing local service providers, possibly because they weigh local authority and business directories differently. Even in the best category, most of the websites the AI surfaced were different from Google's.

AI engines also disagree with each other. A September 2026 single-author preprint, by the founder of an AI-visibility software company who declares a commercial conflict of interest, [audited ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot, Google and Perplexity](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22655) with 15 fixed commercial prompts on June 6, 2026. For the same prompt, 84.9% of engine pairs shared no cited URL, and 96.4% of the URLs appeared in only one engine. A separate same-engine comparison from June 5 to June 6 found 67.0% average turnover in the cited URLs. Fifteen prompts on one day is a small sample, so we treat it as a pattern rather than a rule.

Answers also change from run to run. A September 2026 [preprint on brand recommendations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16304v1) notes that asking the same question more than once can produce different brands and rankings. That study covered six AI models and five product categories rather than contractors.

The closest data to home service comes from a vendor. MentionedOn, which publishes AI recommendation leaderboards, said in an [August 2026 press release](https://www.mycarrollcountynews.com/online_features/press_releases/article_73d2b966-0d0b-50ba-86b9-c86c84454c30.html) that it covered 840 local-service leaderboards across 21 trades in 40 U.S. metro areas. In that data, AI assistants named an average of 9.2 businesses per trade, but only three of those businesses received 56.2% of all mentions. It also said HVAC was among the categories where the AI answers named almost entirely independent local businesses. The release does not name the two assistants it used, and MentionedOn says it chose its trades and metros itself, so they are not a random sample of U.S. local businesses and its shares describe only this data set rather than the whole country.

Consumers say they are using these tools for local picks more often. BrightLocal, which sells local SEO and AI-visibility tools, surveyed 1,002 U.S. adults for its [2026 Local Consumer Review Survey](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/). It found that use of ChatGPT and other AI tools for local recommendations rose from 6% the year before to 45%, making these tools the third most popular source of business recommendations.

**Key Takeaway:** AI search often surfaces different local websites than Google does, different engines rarely agree, and answers change between runs, so one screenshot of one answer proves very little.

## What Do Google, Microsoft, OpenAI and Perplexity Say About GEO?

The companies behind these tools publish guidance, and it is more modest than most GEO pitches. Google's [guide to succeeding in its generative AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide), last updated July 10, 2026, says "The best practices for SEO continue to be relevant because our generative AI features on Google Search are rooted in our core Search ranking and quality systems." It adds, "From Google Search's perspective, optimizing for generative AI search is optimizing for the search experience, and thus still SEO."

The guide is now direct about shortcuts. It tells site owners to put effective SEO ahead of what it calls AEO/GEO hacks, and says that for Google Search you can ignore tactics like chunking content, creating unnecessary AI text files such as llms.txt, or pursuing inauthentic mentions. It says you don't need new machine-readable files, AI text files, markup or Markdown to appear in Google Search. It also says structured data isn't required for generative AI search and there is no special schema.org markup you need to add. Our post on [llms.txt for local businesses](/blog/llms-txt-for-local-business) covers that file in more detail.

What Google asks for instead is original work. The guide says "Creating content that people find unique, compelling, and useful will likely influence your website's presence in generative AI search in the long run more than any of the other suggestions in this guide." It gives "7 Tips for First-Time Homebuyers" as an example of commodity content, which it describes as often based on common knowledge that could come from anyone and as typically adding little unique insight for readers. For a contractor, we read that as a push toward pages written from real jobs and the questions real customers ask.

A separate Google page on [AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features) says "There are no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode, nor other special optimizations necessary." To be shown as a supporting link, a page must be indexed and eligible to appear in Google Search with a snippet.

Microsoft's guidance differs in the details. A [Microsoft Advertising blog post from October 2025](https://about.ads.microsoft.com/en/blog/post/october-2025/optimizing-your-content-for-inclusion-in-ai-search-answers) says "While there's no secret sauce that guarantees selection in AI answers, there are clear practices that improve eligibility." It says traditional SEO is still essential, with crawlability, metadata and internal linking as the baseline. It also recommends structuring content with schema, clear headings and modular layouts, and making answers easy to lift out with short, self-contained phrasing in lists, Q&As and tables.

Google says structured data isn't required and chunking can be ignored for Google Search, while Microsoft recommends schema and modular structure. These are two platforms with two positions, and each one describes its own search. If someone sells you schema work, ask which platform it is meant to help.

OpenAI and Perplexity say less, and what they say is about access. OpenAI's [crawler documentation](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/bots) says "OAI-SearchBot is used to surface websites in search results in ChatGPT's search features." It recommends allowing that crawler in your robots.txt file, which tells crawlers what they may visit. The page also says, "Sites that are opted out of OAI-SearchBot will not be shown in ChatGPT search answers, though can still appear as navigational links."

The same OpenAI page says search inclusion and training are separate settings. A site can allow OAI-SearchBot to appear in search results while blocking GPTBot to signal that its content should not be used to train OpenAI's foundation models. Perplexity's [bot documentation](https://docs.perplexity.ai/guides/bots) gives the same robots.txt advice for PerplexityBot.

**Key Takeaway:** Google tells owners to ignore AEO and GEO hacks and write original content, Microsoft recommends structure and schema while saying there is no secret sauce, and OpenAI and Perplexity ask only for crawler access.

## Can You Measure How Your Business Shows Up in AI Search?

Google says its AI features now reach a very large audience. In a [June 2026 announcement](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/search/new-opportunities-control-and-insights-for-website-owners/), Google said "AI Overviews now has over 2.5 billion monthly active users, while AI Mode has surpassed one billion monthly users." Those are Google's own figures. Going into 2027, Google and Microsoft both report some AI data back to site owners.

Microsoft introduced [AI Performance in Bing Webmaster Tools](https://blogs.bing.com/webmaster/February-2026/Introducing-AI-Performance-in-Bing-Webmaster-Tools-Public-Preview) as a public preview on February 10, 2026. It shows how publisher content appears across Microsoft Copilot, AI-generated summaries in Bing and select partner integrations. On June 16, 2026, Microsoft [added four features](https://blogs.bing.com/search/June-2026/New-AI-Visibility-Insights-in-Bing-Webmaster-Tools-Intents-Topics-Citation-Share-Compare) in preview: Intents, Topics, Citation Share and Compare.

Intents sorts the queries behind AI answers into categories that include Local. Citation Share shows how much of the citation space your site receives for a specific query. Microsoft describes Citation Share as an observational metric, not a ranking system or a competitive scoreboard.

Google Search Console has a [generative AI performance report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/16984139) with impressions for AI Overviews and AI Mode. Google says it rolled these insights out to all websites worldwide as of August 31, 2026. Google says the same about its [Search generative AI control](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/16908024), which lets a site choose whether its content can appear in those features.

If you exclude your site with that control, Google says "You won't receive any traffic or impressions from these features." Google says the control isn't used as a ranking signal for other parts of Search. It also doesn't affect AI training, which Google handles through a separate setting called Google-Extended.

OpenAI's and Perplexity's crawler pages describe no similar report for site owners. For those assistants, the only way to see what they say is to ask them and record the answers.

Clicks are a weak way to judge this work. A [Pew Research Center study](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/) published in July 2025 used March 2025 browsing data from 900 U.S. adults. Google users clicked a regular result in 8% of visits when an AI summary appeared, compared with 15% of visits when none appeared. That data is from early 2025, so treat it as a dated signal.

We expect 2027 to be the first full year that many owners can check their Google and Bing AI visibility with their own data instead of a vendor's screenshots. In our view, any GEO pitch you hear in 2027 should report from these tools where they apply, and from calls and booked jobs.

**Key Takeaway:** Google Search Console and Bing Webmaster Tools now report AI data to site owners while OpenAI's and Perplexity's crawler pages describe no such report, so plan to judge GEO work in 2027 on first-party reports and booked jobs.

## How to Judge a GEO Pitch

Most GEO pitches sound confident, and the evidence above gives you a way to test that confidence. These are the claims worth pushing back on before you sign anything.

- **A guaranteed mention inside ChatGPT or another assistant.** Microsoft says there is no secret sauce that guarantees selection in AI answers, and the studies above found answers change between runs of the same question.
- **A special AI file, chunked layout or schema package sold as the way into Google's AI.** Google says you can ignore chunking and unnecessary AI text files for Google Search, and that structured data isn't required. Microsoft does recommend schema for its own surfaces, so ask which platform the work is for.
- **Seeded mentions on forums or review sites.** Google says seeking inauthentic mentions across the web isn't as helpful as it might seem, and its guide lists pursuing them among the hacks to ignore. Our post on [real SEO vs. fake SEO](/blog/real-seo-vs-fake-seo) covers the link schemes Google's spam rules discount.
- **One screenshot offered as proof.** Ask for the full question list, the dates, the engines checked and the misses, because answers vary by engine and by run.
- **The 40% research figure quoted as a forecast.** That number came from a lab benchmark in which pages already sat in Google's top five, and a later peer-reviewed benchmark found most rewriting methods largely ineffective.
- **A separate AI package priced on top of SEO and local work.** Ask which tasks differ from accurate listings, review work and pages that answer real questions.

A pitch worth hearing starts with the basics. It covers a complete Business Profile, [one name, address and phone number used everywhere](/blog/why-consistent-nap-info-is-crucial-for-local-seo-success), a steady flow of recent reviews and a site that lets AI search crawlers in. ## What Should a Home Service Owner Do First?

GEO has research behind a narrow claim: specific, well-sourced pages can earn more space in AI answers once an engine has found them. The platforms point owners back to crawler access and useful content, and Google's guide calls optimizing for its generative AI features SEO. If you only have time for a few steps before 2027, take them in this order.

1. **Let the AI search crawlers in.** Check that your robots.txt file allows OAI-SearchBot and PerplexityBot. Decide on purpose whether to block GPTBot, since OpenAI treats search and training as separate settings.
2. **Open the AI reports you already have.** Look at the generative AI performance report in Google Search Console and AI Performance in Bing Webmaster Tools. Confirm your Search generative AI control is set the way you want it.
3. **Complete your Google Business Profile and keep reviews coming.** [Google says](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091) "Businesses with complete and accurate info are more likely to show up in local search results," and that more reviews and positive ratings can help local ranking.
4. **Write service pages from real jobs.** Use titles and paragraphs that match the questions homeowners ask. Within a site, a 2026 study of ChatGPT, Gemini and Perplexity answers found that pages whose title and paragraphs matched the questions people ask were cited more.
5. **Test AI answers the right way.** Ask the same questions on several assistants on different days, and write down who was named, which sources were cited and when your business was missing.
6. **Judge the work on calls and booked jobs.** Clicks and screenshots tell you little about whether search work is paying off.
