HVAC SEO is the work of getting a heating and cooling company found when a homeowner searches Google for help. In 2027 that means three places: the map pack, the regular results and the AI answers Google shows on some searches. Google's [help page on local ranking](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en) says "Local results are mainly based on relevance, distance, and popularity." Google's [guide to optimizing for generative AI search](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide) adds that optimizing for AI search "is optimizing for the search experience, and thus still SEO."

In our view, most of what ranked in 2026 still ranks in 2027. The core is still the same: a verified, complete Business Profile, service pages built on details only a working contractor knows, and a steady flow of reviews gathered inside Google's rules. What's new is a Search Console setting and report for AI features, Google's plan to let people ask it to call home repair businesses for them, and rule and tax changes that homeowners are searching about.

ASP is a done-for-you marketing agency for HVAC, plumbing, roofing, remodeling and other home service companies. This guide is for an established HVAC company without an in-house marketing person. It sticks to what Google and government agencies have published, and we label our own experience and our 2027 expectations as ours. If the terms are new, our [comparison of SEO, PPC, AEO and GEO](/blog/seo-vs-ppc-vs-aeo-vs-geo) explains how they fit together.

## How Does Google Rank HVAC Companies in the Map Pack?

By the map pack, we mean the map and short list of Business Profiles in [Google's local results](/blog/local-seo-for-home-service-businesses). Google's local ranking page sums up how that list gets picked: "Local results are mainly based on relevance, distance, and popularity." Further down, the same page explains each factor under its own heading, and the third heading reads Prominence.

Relevance, in Google's words, "is how well a Business Profile matches what someone is searching for." Distance "refers to how far each business is from the customer who's searching." Under Prominence, Google says "Prominent places are more likely to show up in search results." It adds that this factor is "based on info like how many websites link to your business and how many reviews you have."

You cannot change distance, so the work goes into relevance and prominence. Google says "Businesses with complete and accurate info are more likely to show up in local search results." It also says "There's no way to request or pay for a better local ranking on Google." For an HVAC company, we read "complete" as the right primary category, every service you want calls for, accurate hours, a real service area and photos of your own trucks and crews.

![Three cards for Google's local ranking factors: relevance and prominence, which an HVAC company can work on, and distance, which it cannot change.](/images/blog/hvac-seo-map-pack-factors.svg)

*Two of the three factors respond to work on your profile, your reviews and the links to your business.*

### Google Business Profile rules to check first

Start with verification. Under "Verify your business," Google's local ranking page says verifying "tells Google that you're authorized to represent the business, so it's more likely to show up in search results." Verification is also required if you want to opt out of automated calls from Google through your Business Profile settings, which we cover later in this guide.

Google's [Business Profile guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177?hl=en) set the rules for the rest of the profile. "Including unnecessary information in your business name isn't permitted, and could result in the suspension of your Business Profile." That rules out a name like "Smith Heating & Cooling Best AC Repair Dallas." Google also asks businesses to "Use as few categories as possible to describe your overall core business from the provided list," and not to use categories "solely as keywords."

If your company serves customers at their homes, the service-area rules apply. Google says "If you're a service-area business, you should hide your business address from customers." The service area "shouldn't extend farther than about 2 hours of driving time from where your business is based," though Google adds that larger areas may be appropriate for some businesses.

**Key Takeaway:** You cannot move your business closer to every customer, but a verified, complete profile that follows Google's rules helps relevance, and steady reviews and links build prominence.

## Service Pages That Match How Homeowners Search

In our view, [your website also supports relevance](/blog/gbp-vs-website-home-service-priority). A homeowner searching "heat pump installation" and one searching "AC blowing warm air" want different things, and in our experience a single "Heating and Cooling Services" page cannot answer both well. We decide which services get their own page by demand first, then by what a booked job in that service is worth, then by how strong the pages already ranking are. A service with steady searches and good margin earns a full page, while a service nobody searches for by name can live inside a broader page.

In our view, what goes on the page decides whether it ranks. Google's guide to [helpful, people-first content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) asks whether your content shows "first-hand expertise and a depth of knowledge." Google's AI optimization guide draws the same line, using a real estate example. It calls "7 Tips for First-Time Homebuyers" commodity content that "typically adds little unique insight for readers." It holds up "Why We Waived the Inspection & Saved Money: A Look Inside the Sewer Line" as content that "provides unique expert or experienced takes that go beyond common knowledge and the ordinary."

For an HVAC company, first-hand detail is what only a working contractor knows. That includes how your techs tell a simple repair from a failing system, when a repair stops making sense on older equipment, and what drives the price of an install in your market. It also includes what gets checked first on a service call. Those details come from you or a senior tech, which is why we interview for them instead of writing from what other sites already say.

### City pages and pages for every search variation

Google's helpful content guide lists warning signs. One is "producing lots of content on many different topics in hopes that some of it might perform well in search results." Another is "using extensive automation to produce content on many topics." Google's [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) give two examples of doorway abuse that fit HVAC city pages: "Creating substantially similar pages that are closer to search results than a clearly defined, browseable hierarchy," and "Having multiple domain names or pages targeted at specific regions or cities that funnel users to one page."

Google's AI guide applies the same warning to AI search. It talks about "fan-out queries," the related searches Google's AI model runs to gather more results for an answer. Writing separate content for every search variation or fan-out query "primarily to manipulate rankings or generative AI responses in Google Search violates Google's scaled content abuse spam policy." In our view, that rules out copying one city page thirty times with the town name swapped. It also rules out separate pages for "AC repair cost," "AC repair price" and "how much is AC repair."

A city page earns its place only when it says something true about that area, such as the age of the homes you work on there or the permit process in that county. Our post on [what Google wants from local content in 2027](/blog/what-google-wants-from-local-content-2027) goes deeper on that.

Pages also have to be readable by Google. To appear as a supporting link in AI Overviews or AI Mode, Google's [page on AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features) says "a page must be indexed and eligible to be shown in Google Search with a snippet." [We check indexing, mobile load speed and redirects](/seo) before writing anything new, because Google cannot show a page it cannot read.

**Key Takeaway:** Build a real page for each service that earns you money, fill it with what only someone who does the work would know, and skip pages that exist only to catch another search variation.

## How Should an HVAC Company Ask for Google Reviews?

Google's local ranking page says "More reviews and positive ratings can help your business's local ranking." In our experience, reviews also do the selling once a homeowner is looking at the list. We look at review volume and recency together, because a pile of reviews from years ago describes a business that may have changed since.

The practical move is to make the request part of closing every job. Google's [review tips](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122?hl=en) suggest asking customers "to visit a Google link or scan a QR code," which fits on an invoice, a leave-behind card or a follow-up text. The same page says, "As best practice, reply to reviews and share your perspective." In our view, a calm, specific reply to a bad review shows the next reader how your company handles a problem.

Your service list feeds your reviews too. Google's page on [reviews for service businesses](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9950987?hl=en) says customers "may be asked to identify what tasks your business performed." It explains where those tasks come from: "Available tasks come from the merchant's service list, website (if available), or the standard list of tasks based on the business type or other authoritative sources." Google says "only certain service businesses" get aspect ratings collected, and the page does not say whether HVAC is one of them. If a job type is missing, Google says merchants "can add it to their service list on Business Profile Manager."

### What Google and the FTC ban

Google's rule on incentives covers more than paying for a good review. Its review tips page states the rule in full. "Offering incentives, like free or discounted goods or services, in exchange for customers to post reviews, change reviews, or remove negative reviews is considered fake & misleading content and is strictly prohibited." A discount for updating or removing a bad review breaks that rule just as a discount for a five-star review does.

Google's [prohibited content policy](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7400114?hl=en) also covers how you ask. Merchants may not "selectively solicit positive reviews from customers," so the review link cannot go only to happy customers. The same page says merchants "should not require or pressure users to leave ratings or write reviews while on the premises, nor should they request that specific content be included." Its examples include staff asked to solicit "a certain number of reviews" and reviews "that include specific content, including content that identifies a staff member." In our view, that makes "please mention Mike by name" a line to drop from your review request.

The Federal Trade Commission [announced a final rule banning fake reviews](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials) in August 2024. According to the [rule text](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/text/2024/08/22/2024-18519.txt), it took effect on October 21, 2024. The rule bars a business from offering incentives "conditioned expressly or by implication on, the writing or creation of consumer reviews expressing a particular sentiment, whether positive or negative." A "$25 off for a five-star review" card breaks both Google's rules and the FTC's.

Breaking Google's rules has a cost. Google's page on [Business Profile restrictions](https://support.google.com/business/answer/14114287?hl=en) says that if an owner violates its Fake Engagement policy, Google "might place restrictions on their Business Profile (in addition to removing the violative reviews)." Its examples include a profile that cannot receive new reviews for a set period, existing reviews unpublished for a set period, and a warning telling consumers that fake reviews were removed.

Google also documents [negative review extortion scams](https://support.google.com/business/answer/16404809?hl=en). It says they "may involve a sudden increase in 1-star and 2-star reviews on your Google Business Profile." Someone then demands money, goods or services to remove the negative reviews. Google's advice is "Do not engage with or pay the malicious individuals," because paying "doesn't guarantee the removal of reviews."

If you run Local Services Ads, the same reviews count there. Google's [Local Services help](https://support.google.com/localservices/answer/7496631?hl=en) says "Star ratings and number of reviews affect how your business is ranked within Local Services Ads." It also says "Your customer reviews are managed through Google Business Profile Manager, not your Local Service Ads account." That does not make ad spend a map pack factor, because Google says there is no way to pay for a better local ranking.

![Diagram: Google Business Profile reviews count toward map pack ranking and Local Services Ads ranking; service-business reviews can include the job type.](/images/blog/hvac-seo-reviews-feed.svg)
*One set of reviews, managed in Business Profile, counts toward local ranking and Local Services Ads ranking, and service-business reviews can record the job you did.*

**Key Takeaway:** Ask every customer for a review the same way and reply to what comes in. Never offer a discount for posting, changing or removing a review, never set staff review quotas, and never ask for specific wording.

## When Should an HVAC Company Publish Seasonal Content?

ENERGY STAR ties HVAC check-ups to the seasons. Its [heating and cooling maintenance checklist](https://www.energystar.gov/saveathome/heating-cooling/maintenance-checklist) tells homeowners, "Contractors get busy once summer and winter come, so it's best to check the cooling system in the spring and the heating system in the fall." The same calendar tells you when your content has to be ready.

In our experience, a new page takes months to start bringing in calls, and no one can promise a fixed timeline. That is ASP's view from our own work, not a rule Google publishes. So we plan cooling pages and AC maintenance content through the winter, and heating content through the summer. When a season comes back around, we update last year's seasonal pages with fresh details and dates instead of starting new pages that compete with the old ones.

![Timeline showing cooling pages built in winter before spring check-ups, and heating pages built in summer before fall check-ups.](/images/blog/hvac-seo-seasonal-calendar.svg)

*Each content block sits one season ahead of the check-ups and busy months it serves.*

Plan for one change to the source itself in 2027. EPA's [ENERGY STAR page](https://www.epa.gov/vcs/energy-star) says EPA and the Department of Energy signed an agreement on March 3, 2026 "to consolidate leadership and program administration at DOE." It adds, "A complete transition of the ENERGY STAR program, including the IT Portfolio and associated databases, will occur by July 2027." If your seasonal pages link to ENERGY STAR, we expect some of those links could move during 2027, so check them each season.

Your profile should follow the season too. Google's Business Profile guidelines say "If applicable, you may use your current seasonal hours as your regular hours." Update them when your schedule changes.

**Key Takeaway:** Publish and refresh seasonal pages a season early, because ENERGY STAR steers homeowners to spring cooling and fall heating check-ups.

## Which HVAC Topics Should Your Website Explain in 2027?

Three dated government changes give HVAC companies something useful to explain in 2027. When we checked Google's autocomplete on November 23, 2026, suggestions included "is r410a being phased out," "new ac refrigerant 2026" and "heat pump tax credit 2026." Autocomplete is not search volume, and the suggestions we saw leaned toward Colorado. Still, they show what some homeowners are typing. Each topic below rests on a dated government source, and in our view your page should cite that source too.

### What changed in EPA's refrigerant rules?

EPA's [table of HFC restrictions by sector](https://www.epa.gov/hfcs/technology-transitions-hfc-restrictions-sector) lists "Residential and light commercial air conditioning and heat pump systems (e.g., mini-splits, unitary systems)" with a global warming potential (GWP) limit of 700, starting January 1, 2025. On May 21, 2026, EPA [finalized a rule](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2026-05/fact-sheet-san-12166-technology-transitions-frm.pdf) revising its 2023 Technology Transitions Rule. For residential AC and heat pump systems, EPA's fact sheet lists this change.

"Removes the January 1, 2026, installation compliance date for equipment using refrigerants above the 700 limit that were domestically manufactured or imported into the United States before January 1, 2025."

The sector table now says the same thing in a footnote: "No installation compliance date for systems where all specified components were manufactured or imported prior to January 1, 2025." Another footnote covers repairs: "Components used to repair existing systems are not subject to these restrictions."

When we checked, EPA's own frequent-questions page still carried an older line about 2026 installs. Cite the May 2026 rule and the sector table instead. For your website, we would answer three questions in your own words. What changed for new systems in 2025? What did the May 2026 rule change for older equipment? And what does any of it mean for a homeowner repairing an existing system?

### Is there a heat pump tax credit in 2026 or 2027?

Not the federal one most homeowners remember. For the Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit, known as 25C, the IRS [FAQ on the 2025 law changes](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/faqs-for-modification-of-sections-25c-25d-25e-30c-30d-45l-45w-and-179d-under-public-law-119-21-139-stat-72-july-4-2025-commonly-known-as-the-one-big-beautiful-bill-obbb) says "The credit will not be allowed for any property placed in service after December 31, 2025." The IRS [credit page](https://www.irs.gov/credits-deductions/energy-efficient-home-improvement-credit) says "You can claim the credit for improvements made through December 31, 2025." Before it ended, qualifying heat pumps could earn "a credit up to $2,000 per year."

Autocomplete still suggests "heat pump tax credit 2026" and "heat pump tax credit 2026 deadline." In our view, that is a clear content opportunity. A short page can explain what ended, when it ended and what a homeowner installing in 2027 should ask about instead. We have not covered state or utility rebates here, and they need their own sources before you write about them.

### What is happening with the furnace efficiency standard?

DOE's [Federal Register notice](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-04-27/html/2026-08145.htm) from April 27, 2026 says the final rule set standards for non-weatherized gas furnaces and mobile home gas furnaces at 95% AFUE (annual fuel utilization efficiency). Those are "levels that effectively require the use of condensing technology." The notice says "The compliance date for the amended energy conservation standards for the subject consumer furnaces is December 18, 2028."

The same notice says that on February 19, 2026, DOE received a petition from three gas industry groups. They are the American Gas Association, the American Public Gas Association and the National Propane Gas Association. The petition asked DOE "to amend the compliance dates" for this rule and one for commercial water heating equipment. When we checked in November 2026, we did not find a final DOE decision on the petition. Write about December 18, 2028 as the current date, say that a delay has been requested, and update the page if DOE acts.

**Key Takeaway:** Use 2027 content to explain dated changes homeowners are still searching about: the refrigerant limit and EPA's May 2026 rule, the end of the federal 25C credit and the furnace standard still set for December 18, 2028.

## Can Google's AI Mode Call Your HVAC Company?

Google has announced calls for home repair businesses, but we have not found Google saying HVAC is included. In its [I/O 2026 Search announcement](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/search/search-io-2026/) on May 19, 2026, Google wrote: "And for select categories like home repair, beauty or pet care, you can ask Google to call businesses on your behalf. These capabilities will roll out to everyone in the U.S. this summer." We have not seen Google confirm that the rollout finished.

Google's [AI Mode help page](https://support.google.com/websearch/answer/17104441?hl=en) explains when a call happens. "If a business does not have its information listed online, such as the earliest available appointment or current inventory in stock, Google will make a call to get those answers for you." The page's examples include "Research available home services, such as electricians, tree cutting, or exterior cleaning." HVAC is not on that list.

On the business side, Google's page on [automated calls and texts](https://support.google.com/business/answer/16190256?hl=en) says "Google might occasionally use automated calls or texts to contact the phone number on your Business Profile." Customers can ask Google to confirm "the pricing and availability of in-store products and services from nearby local businesses," and "Google may call your business to confirm the details." If you share that information, "the customer receives an email or SMS text that includes your business's product details or service quote." Google says "the feature is available in all states except Indiana, Louisiana, Minnesota, Montana, and Nebraska."

You can opt out. Google says "If you don't want your business to receive automated calls or texts from Google, you can tell us over the phone or change your Business Profile settings." For the settings route, it adds, "If you haven't verified your business, you must do so in order to opt out." In our view, the choice depends on who answers your phone. If your office can handle a pricing question from an automated caller accurately, staying in may put your quote in front of the homeowner. If nobody can answer those calls well, opting out may be the safer choice.

### Emergency searches and your Business Profile

In our experience, a homeowner with no heat on a January night rarely reads blog posts or compares five companies' service pages. They are more likely to look at the map, check the rating and call. That is our view, not a published study. Either way, [the phone number](/blog/why-consistent-nap-info-is-crucial-for-local-seo-success), hours and services on your profile have to be right, both for those homeowners and for any call Google makes for them.

Google's guidelines ask businesses to "Provide your regular customer-facing hours of operation." In our view, list after-hours service only if a person or a real answering process picks up the phone. On the website, an emergency page should make the phone number easy to tap and say clearly which problems you treat as urgent. Avoid promising a response time unless you can meet it every time, in every season.

**Key Takeaway:** Google has announced that people can ask it to call home repair businesses for them, so keep your profile's phone number, hours and services accurate, and decide on purpose whether to stay in or opt out.

## Do AI Overviews and AI Mode Need a Separate HVAC SEO Plan?

No. Google's AI optimization guide says "From Google Search's perspective, optimizing for generative AI search is optimizing for the search experience, and thus still SEO." It also explains why. "The best practices for SEO continue to be relevant because our generative AI features on Google Search are rooted in our core Search ranking and quality systems." Our guide on [how to rank in AI search](/blog/how-to-rank-in-ai-search) covers this in more depth, and our post asking [whether GEO works](/blog/does-geo-work) looks at the GEO side.

There is one setting to check. Google's older page on AI features says "There are no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode, nor other special optimizations necessary." The 2026 guide adds that "a site must be included in Search generative AI features in Search Console to be eligible for display in generative AI features on Google Search." Google's [help page on that control](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/16908024?hl=en) says inclusion "is the default control for all properties." Ask whoever manages your Search Console to confirm nobody has changed it.

Google also lists things you can skip. Its guide says "You don't need to create new machine readable files, AI text files, markup, or Markdown to appear in Google Search (including its generative AI capabilities), as Google Search itself doesn't use them." Our post on [llms.txt for local businesses](/blog/llms-txt-for-local-business) covers that file in detail. Google adds, "There's no requirement to break your content into tiny pieces for AI to better understand it." It also says "seeking inauthentic "mentions" across the web isn't as helpful as it might seem."

Be careful with vendors here too. Google warns, "Be wary of third-party tools that promise ranking success or claim to use "internal" Google metrics. No third-party tool has access to our internal ranking or AI systems." Nobody can promise an HVAC company a spot in an AI answer.

Your Business Profile still counts in AI answers. Google says "Where appropriate, generative AI responses can include product listings, product information, and information about local businesses." It adds that Business Profiles "can help your products and services to be visible in both AI responses and other Google Search results."

### How to see your AI impressions

As of 2026, you can see some of this in Search Console. Google [introduced Search Generative AI performance reports](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2026/06/gen-ai-performance-reports) on June 3, 2026, and a later note says "As of August 31, 2026, we've rolled out these insights to all websites worldwide." Google says, "The new Search Console reports are designed to give you dedicated views of your impressions within generative AI features on Search, such as AI Overviews and AI Mode, as well as generative AI features in Discover." Impressions are "How often URLs from your site appeared in generative AI features in Search and Discover."

Google's announcement lists no click metric for this report, so treat it as a count of appearances, not leads. In our view, calls and booked jobs by source are still the measure that pays the bills. Our [SEO and AEO predictions for 2027](/blog/seo-aeo-predictions-2027) explain why we expect clicks to become a weaker measure.

**Key Takeaway:** Google says optimizing for its AI answers is still SEO, so confirm the Search Console AI setting is on, skip special AI files and treat the new report as an impressions count.

## What Should an HVAC Company Do First in 2027?

The order of work is fairly predictable. If you only have time for a few steps, take them in this order:

1. **Fix anything that stops Google from reading your site.** Check indexing, mobile speed and redirects, and confirm the generative AI setting in Search Console is still on.
2. **Verify and clean up your Business Profile.** Check the name, primary category, services, hours and service area against Google's rules, and decide whether to stay in or opt out of automated calls.
3. **Make the review request part of how every job closes.** Use a Google link or QR code, reply to reviews and keep incentives, quotas and requested wording out of it.
4. **Build or rebuild the pages for your highest-value services.** Fill them with what your techs know, and skip near-duplicate city or keyword pages.
5. **Plan seasonal content one season ahead.** Add 2027 pages on the refrigerant rules, the end of the federal 25C credit and the furnace standard.
6. **Measure calls and booked jobs by source.** Use the Generative AI report as an impressions check, not as a lead count.
