SEO is not dead, but it has changed. AI summaries now answer some searches at the top of Google, and fewer people click through on those searches. Google says its AI features are built on the same ranking and quality systems as regular search, so the work that earns a ranking is still the work that gets a business shown. What changed is how many clicks a good page produces and how you should measure the results.

ASP is a growth-systems marketing agency for home service operators, and [our SEO work](/seo) follows Google's published guidance for its AI features. Below is what independent research found about clicks, what Google says about its own AI features, and what a business should keep doing, stop doing and start tracking. The examples lean toward HVAC, plumbing, roofing and electrical companies, but the same logic holds for most local businesses.

## Why Business Owners Are Asking If SEO Is Dead

The question comes from a visible change on the results page. On a share of searches, Google now places an AI-generated summary above the regular links, and people are more likely to end their browsing session on those pages. The [Pew Research Center studied the browsing data of 900 U.S. adults](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/) during March 2025. Some 18% of the Google searches in that study produced an AI summary.

On searches with a summary, users clicked a regular search result in 8% of visits. On searches without one, they clicked nearly twice as often, in 15% of visits. Clicks on links inside the summary itself were rare, happening in just 1% of visits to pages with a summary. People also ended their browsing session on 26% of pages with a summary, compared with 16% of pages with only regular results.

For any business that depends on search traffic, those numbers mean fewer clicks on the searches where a summary shows up. They do not show that search stopped working. People still ran every one of those searches, and Google still chose which pages to rank and which sources to draw from. The businesses whose pages Google trusts are still the ones most likely to be in front of the searcher, even when fewer people click.

**Key Takeaway:** In Pew's data, users clicked a regular result in 8% of visits when an AI summary appeared, versus 15% when none did, so plan for fewer clicks per search even when your ranking holds.

## What Google Says About Clicks From AI Search

Google reads its own data differently. In an August 2025 post, [Liz Reid, Google's head of Search, wrote](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/search/ai-search-driving-more-queries-higher-quality-clicks/) that total organic click volume from Google to websites has been relatively stable year over year. She also wrote that Google is sending slightly more quality clicks than a year earlier, which Google defines as clicks where the user does not quickly click back. Google's [documentation on AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features) makes a similar claim, saying clicks from results pages with AI Overviews are higher quality because users are more likely to spend more time on the site.

Pew measured click rates on individual searches, while Google is describing total clicks across all of Search, so the two findings are not direct contradictions. The same Google post also says user trends are shifting traffic to different sites, with some sites losing traffic and others gaining it. Each source has limits. Pew's study covers one month of browsing from a 900-person panel, and Google's figures come from Google itself.

For a home service owner, the takeaway is that traffic is moving between sites. Reid wrote that people are more likely to click into web content that helps them learn more, such as an in-depth review, an original post, a unique perspective or a thoughtful first-person analysis. A business whose pages offer none of that is more likely to land on the losing side.

**Key Takeaway:** Google says total clicks held steady while traffic shifted between sites, which puts more weight on whether your pages are the ones worth sending people to.

## What Google Says Its AI Features Run On

The strongest evidence that SEO still works comes from Google itself. Its [guide to optimizing for generative AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide) says the best practices for SEO continue to be relevant because those features are rooted in Google's core Search ranking and quality systems. Google's AI features documentation adds that there are no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode, and no other special optimizations are necessary. To show up as a supporting link, a page must be indexed and eligible to appear in Google Search with a snippet.

Google also says you don't need to create new machine-readable files, AI text files, markup or Markdown to appear in its generative AI features, because Google Search does not use them. The same guide tells site owners not to recycle what others have already said or what a generative AI model could easily produce. Google is asking for original, useful pages that it can crawl, which is what good SEO has asked for all along.

We treat answer engine optimization as SEO held to a higher standard for that reason. It uses the same levers as regular SEO: first-hand detail, named authors, depth on the questions buyers ask, and a site that crawls cleanly. If someone offers to sell you a separate AI layer for Google, keep in mind that Google's own guidance says no special optimization is needed.

**Key Takeaway:** Google's documentation says its AI answers draw on the same ranking systems as regular search, and a page that is not indexed and snippet-eligible cannot appear as a supporting link in them.

## What Changed for Home Service Searches

The change hits some searches harder than others. In the Pew study, just 8% of one- or two-word searches produced an AI summary, but that share rose to 53% for searches with 10 words or more. Searches that began with question words such as "who," "what," "when" or "why" produced a summary 60% of the time.

Homeowners run both kinds of searches. A long question about why a furnace keeps shutting off, or whether a slow drain can wait until Monday, looks like the searches most likely to get a summary in Pew's data. A short search for a trade and a town looks more like the searches least likely to get one. Research questions are where you should expect the fewest clicks. For "near me" and map-pack searches, your Business Profile and reviews carry real weight.

Local results still follow rules Google publishes. Google's help page on [improving your local ranking](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091) says local results are mainly based on relevance, distance and popularity, and that more reviews and positive ratings can help a business's local ranking. Google's AI guide also says a Google Business Profile can help your services be visible in both AI responses and regular search results. Our [local SEO playbook](/blog/local-seo-for-home-service-businesses) covers that side in detail, and our comparison of [your Google Business Profile and your website](/blog/gbp-vs-website-home-service-priority) walks through which one to fund first.

Assistants outside Google, such as ChatGPT and Perplexity, are a separate question. They do not publish how they pick which local business to name, and we don't claim to know. We cover that side, and what can be measured there, on our [generative engine optimization page](/geo).

**Key Takeaway:** Long, question-shaped searches are the ones most likely to trigger an AI summary, so your research content has to be worth citing while your Business Profile and reviews carry the local signals Google names for map results.

## What Still Works and What Stopped Paying Off

Most of the SEO fundamentals held. What stopped paying off was the shortcut work that tried to game rankings instead of earning them. For a home service business in 2026, these are the pieces we consider worth the money:

- **Service pages that answer the search.** Each high-value service gets a page that tells a homeowner what the job involves, what drives the cost and what can go wrong.
- **First-hand detail.** Numbers and failure modes from you or a senior tech are the kind of original material Google's guide asks for.
- **Named, credentialed authors and accurate dates.** Real people with real licenses and years in the trade give a reader a reason to trust the page.
- **Clean technical health.** Pages need to be indexed, load fast on a phone and stay clear of redirect chains.
- **A complete Business Profile and steady reviews.** These are the local signals Google names in its own ranking help.
- **Content depth built over time.** Related pages on cost, comparisons and seasonal problems, linked together, cover a topic more fully than one page can.

On the other side of the ledger, thin pages, bought links and AI text files sold as a way into AI answers are poor uses of a marketing budget. Google says its search does not use AI text files at all. We don't buy links or sell AI files, and we would steer any owner away from paying for them. Our post on [real SEO versus fake SEO](/blog/real-seo-vs-fake-seo) covers what Google's spam rules say about manipulation tactics, and our guide to [Google SEO strategies for home service businesses](/blog/google-seo-strategies-2025) is the longer version of the list above.

**Key Takeaway:** The SEO that survived AI summaries is the slow, first-hand work on your own site and profile, while the tactics that faded were built to fool a ranking system.

## How to Measure SEO When Fewer People Click

If summaries answer more questions without a click, traffic alone undercounts what your SEO is doing. Google's documentation says sites appearing in AI features such as AI Overviews and AI Mode are included in the overall search traffic in Search Console. Google has also added a [generative AI performance report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/16984139) to Search Console, which includes impressions for AI Overviews and AI Mode. Google says it rolled the report out to all websites worldwide as of August 31, 2026. If you don't see it, your site may not have enough impressions in these features yet.

Impressions show that Google is putting your pages in front of people, but they do not pay the bills. Rankings and impressions are leading indicators, and calls, form fills and booked jobs tell you whether the work paid. Getting that answer means tracking each lead from the search that produced it to the job in your CRM, where your CRM supports it. Our guide to [marketing attribution for home service businesses](/blog/marketing-attribution-home-service-businesses) explains how that tracking fits together.

**Key Takeaway:** Track impressions, including Google's generative AI report if it shows for your property, but judge SEO by the calls and booked jobs it produces.

## Is SEO Dead in 2026?

SEO lost some of its easy clicks, and it lost the shortcuts that used to work against weak competition. It did not lose its foundation. Google says the systems behind its AI answers are rooted in the same core ranking and quality systems that rank regular results, and it asks for what good SEO has always delivered: original, useful pages that it can crawl and index. For a home service business, the plan is to keep building service pages and research content worth citing, keep your Business Profile and reviews current, and measure the work against booked jobs instead of raw traffic.
