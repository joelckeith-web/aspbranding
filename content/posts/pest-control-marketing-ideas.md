The best pest control marketing ideas make your company easy to find and easy to trust when a homeowner nearby needs help. In practice that means a complete Google Business Profile, a service page for each pest you want more of, paid search that covers the gap while organic rankings build, and tracking that shows which channel booked the job.

You are not the only company chasing those homeowners. The Bureau of Labor Statistics counted 17,606 private pest control establishments in the US in 2024. This guide covers the main pest control marketing strategies for an established company. It draws on what Google and federal agencies have published, and on how ASP runs [pest control marketing](/industries/pest-control) for owners who want booked jobs, not just leads.

## Planning Marketing by Service Line

Pest control covers more ground than most trades. The [federal industry definition](https://www.census.gov/naics/resources/model/dataHandler.php?input=561710&chart=2022&search=Y) includes controlling birds, mosquitoes, rodents, termites and other insects and pests, and it counts fumigation too. A termite inspection and a mosquito treatment reach different buyers with different ticket sizes, so one generic message rarely fits both.

Plan each service line on its own. Decide which pests you want more of, then build pages, ads and offers for those first. We rank pages by what a booked job is worth to you rather than by search volume alone, so a service with steady demand and good margin goes to the top of the list.

A strong service page can grow into a cluster: the main page plus a cost breakdown, a treatment comparison and the seasonal problem, all linked together. A pest nobody searches for by name can be folded into a broader page instead of getting a thin page of its own. Our [pest control SEO](/seo/pest-control) page shows how that work gets scoped.

**Key Takeaway:** Choose the pests you want more of and give each one its own page and message, starting with the jobs worth the most to your business.

## Local Search and Reviews

Homeowners shopping for pest control pick from the companies they find nearby, so local search belongs in every plan. On the map side, Business Profile data, review volume and recency, and citation consistency carry real weight. ASP's [Local SEO Pro](/local-seo-pro) covers that work with profile management, citation audits across 60+ directories and a ranking heatmap from 50+ grid points a month.

Since recency counts along with volume, build review requests into your follow-up so every customer gets the same request by text or email, with a direct link to your Google review page.

Keep the process inside Google's review guidelines and the FTC's rule on fake reviews. Never offer a discount or gift for a review, never give your techs review quotas and never script what a customer should say. Our [local SEO playbook for home service businesses](/blog/local-seo-for-home-service-businesses) goes deeper on the profile itself.

**Key Takeaway:** Keep the profile complete and the reviews recent, and ask every customer the same way without incentives.

## Local Services Ads and Google Search Ads

Google lists pest control services among its eligible US [Local Services Ads categories](https://support.google.com/localservices/answer/12174778?hl=en). These ads charge per lead rather than per click, and they appear above the map results. Google screens every business before ads run.

Where you show inside Local Services Ads is decided by an auction. Google's [help page on ad rankings](https://support.google.com/localservices/answer/7527305?hl=en) says the auction weighs your bid and your profile quality. Rating, number of reviews, average response time, photos and completed verification checks all feed profile quality.

The same page says missed calls may hurt your responsiveness, so answered phones belong on the same list as the bid. Our piece on [speed to lead](/blog/speed-to-lead-home-service-businesses) looks at what a missed call costs a home service business.

Google Search Ads cover what Local Services Ads can't. They catch the homeowner typing a specific problem and let you target the services you want by name. Build one ad group per service or service area, use phrase match and add to the negative keyword list every week. LSA inventory is capped per metro, so most companies that take paid search seriously run both, as our [pest control advertising](/ppc/pest-control) page explains.

**Key Takeaway:** Run Local Services Ads if you pass screening, back them with answered calls and fresh reviews, and use Search Ads to target the specific pests you want.

## Seasonal Demand

Some pest problems follow the calendar. The CDC says ticks are [most active from April through September](https://www.cdc.gov/ticks/prevention/index.html), though people can be exposed year-round. Tick pages should be live and indexed before April, not written once the calls start.

Payroll data is another clue, with clear limits. BLS figures show private pest control employers in the US had 141,809 people on staff in January 2024 and 159,456 in July 2024, about 12% more. That counts workers, not customer calls, and it covers one year, so it can't set your budget calendar on its own.

New problems show up too. According to BLS, the [growing number of invasive insect species](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/building-and-grounds-cleaning/pest-control-workers.htm) is expected to create demand for pest control services. When a new pest starts appearing in your area, a clear page on what it is and how you treat it gives local searchers a straight answer from a local company.

Neither the tick months nor the payroll figures measure ad demand directly. Once tracking is in place, set the budget and the ad schedule from your booked jobs by month, and publish seasonal content ahead of the months your records show are busy.

**Key Takeaway:** Publish seasonal pages before the season starts, and let your own booked-job history decide when budget rises and falls.

## Trust Signals Buyers Check

BLS notes that while some people handle pests themselves, many prefer to hire a professional. When they do, a 2005 EPA [consumer fact sheet](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2015-10/documents/pest-control-company.pdf) gives them questions to ask, including whether the company is licensed, carries appropriate insurance and guarantees its work. For termite work, EPA tells homeowners to [ask to see the company's state license](https://www.epa.gov/safepestcontrol/termites-how-identify-and-control-them).

Your marketing should answer those questions before anyone asks. Put license and insurance details where a buyer can see them on the site and in your ads. State your guarantee terms plainly if you offer one, and leave guarantee language out if you don't.

Named, credentialed authors on your service pages help as well, because they show that a trained person stands behind the advice. A careful buyer who finds those answers up front has fewer reasons to keep shopping.

**Key Takeaway:** Show your license, insurance and any guarantee where buyers look, since EPA's own consumer advice tells them to ask about each one.

## Facebook and Instagram Ads

Meta works for pest control in a few specific ways. It brings back people who read a service page and did not call, and recurring services can carry a seasonal offer directly. Cold ads asking a stranger to book a service call rarely perform for the trades, so they should not be the first thing you fund. Our [Meta ads](/meta-ads) page covers creative and audiences in more detail.

Meta also helps with hiring, which matters once more work starts coming in. BLS counts about 108,700 pest control worker jobs in 2025 and projects employment to grow 6 percent from 2025 to 2035. Run recruiting as its own campaign with its own budget, so hiring spend is never read as lead-generation performance.

**Key Takeaway:** Use Meta for retargeting, seasonal offers on recurring services and hiring, not for cold requests to book.

## Tracking Booked Jobs

Clicks and calls are where a report starts, not where it ends. Following a lead from the first call or form to the booked job takes four connected pieces: call tracking for phone leads, forms that capture campaign tags, your CRM, and the ad platforms that need booked-job data sent back to them. Our guide to [marketing attribution for home service businesses](/blog/marketing-attribution-home-service-businesses) explains how they fit together.

Recurring customers need one more rule. Credit the first job to the channel that won the customer, and count later visits as retention. That way renewals don't inflate the numbers for the channel that brought in the first job.
