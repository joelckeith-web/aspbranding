Residential HVAC demand went into 2026 soft, and industry reporting expected the first half of the year to stay difficult for residential sales. Most operators' first instinct — pull back on marketing to protect cash — is exactly backward. When demand drops, the businesses that keep showing up take share from the ones that disappear. This post covers what's happening with HVAC demand, why a soft market is the time to spend smarter rather than less, how much to budget as a percentage of revenue, and where to put it for the best return.

This isn't a pep talk to spend recklessly. It's the opposite: in a tighter market, every marketing dollar has to be sharper and pointed at the homeowners who are ready to buy. But the operators who go dark to save money usually find that the savings cost them far more in lost pipeline than they ever banked.

## Why residential HVAC demand softened going into 2026

The residential side of the HVAC industry entered 2026 in a slower stretch. In January 2026, ACHR News reported [soft residential demand carrying into the new year](https://www.achrnews.com/articles/165645-soft-residential-demand-carries-into-the-new-year). The article said the first half of 2026 would likely remain difficult for residential sales.

A few forces stacked up at once:

- **Weak new-home construction and historically low existing-home sales** sharply reduced replacement demand.
- **High interest rates and consumer stress** weighed on sales, and more homeowners chose to repair rather than replace.

None of this means the work disappears — people's systems still break, and comfort is non-negotiable in a heat wave. It means the pool of jobs is tighter, and more operators are competing for each one. ACHR News coverage from September 2025 of [residential sales running out of steam](https://www.achrnews.com/articles/165257-residential-hvac-sales-run-out-of-steam) tells the same story.

**Key Takeaway:** Residential HVAC demand went into 2026 soft, driven by weak construction and home sales, high interest rates and repair-over-replace decisions. The work doesn't vanish, but more operators are fighting over a smaller pool of jobs.

## Should HVAC companies spend more on marketing in a slow market?

When the phone slows down, cutting marketing feels responsible. In a soft market, it's often the most expensive decision an operator can make.

When overall demand shrinks, marketing stops being about growing the pie and starts being about winning your slice. Every job is now a head-to-head between you and the competitor down the road. The homeowner with a dying compressor is still searching "AC repair near me" — the only question is who shows up. If you've gone dark to save money, the answer is the competitor who didn't.

There's a longer-term cost, too. The slow market is when disciplined operators take share that's expensive to win back when demand returns. Protecting this month's cash by going dark usually just moves the pain to next quarter's empty pipeline.

**Key Takeaway:** In a soft market, marketing shifts from growing demand to capturing share. The operators who keep showing up win the jobs that the ones who cut back hand over.

## How much to spend under ASP's planning framework

So what's the right number? ASP's planning framework sets marketing spend as a percentage of gross revenue. These tiers are our own planning guide, not an outside benchmark:

- **5–7% — maintain.** Enough to hold your current position and keep the lights on in search. This is the floor, not the goal, in a competitive year.
- **8–10% — healthy growth.** The range most established operators should target to grow steadily while staying profitable.
- **11–15% — aggressive.** For operators going hard after market share — newer businesses, competitive metros, or anyone deliberately using a soft market to take ground while competitors retreat.

A few caveats. These are percentages of *gross* revenue. Newer businesses and those in dense, competitive metros belong at the higher end; established operators with a strong base of repeat and referral work can sit lower. And the right number isn't just a percentage — it depends on your margins, your average ticket, and your growth goal.

That's why we built a tool to help with the math. The [ASP Marketing Budget Calculator](/marketing-budget-calculator) gives you a starting budget range from your revenue, industry and market, so you're setting a budget on purpose instead of guessing.

**Key Takeaway:** ASP's planning framework budgets 5–7% of gross revenue to maintain, 8–10% for healthy growth, and 11–15% to aggressively take share. Adjust for your margins and market — and run your real number rather than guessing.

## Why the right percentage depends on your shop

The tiers give you a starting range. Where your shop lands inside them depends on what a booked job is worth to you, how well known you already are in your market and how much work comes back to you without new marketing. The examples below are illustrative, not client results.

**A shop opening a new service area.** An operator adding a town where few people know its name has few reviews, repeat customers or referrals there to lean on. That points toward the higher end of the framework, at least until the new area starts producing its own repeat work.

**An established shop with steady repeat work.** A company that has served the same area for years and gets a large share of its jobs from repeat and referral customers can often hold the lower end. Its marketing is defending a position more than building one.

**Replacement-heavy versus service-heavy.** Two shops with the same revenue can still need different budgets. A shop that wants more system replacements depends more on winning new customers than on repeat work, which points toward the higher end of the framework. A shop built mostly on service calls and maintenance customers leans more on repeat work from its existing list, which can let it hold a lower percentage. In both cases, the right budget starts from what a booked job is worth to you.

To see what each piece of that budget costs, from agency fees to Local Services Ads leads, read [how much HVAC marketing costs](/blog/hvac-marketing-cost).

**Key Takeaway:** Use the framework as a starting range, then move up or down based on how established you are in your market and what your typical booked job is worth.

## Where to put it in a soft market

A tighter market rewards spending on intent and speed over awareness. Here's where the budget works hardest.

- **Capture ready-to-buy searches.** A fully optimized Google Business Profile and search ads for high-intent queries like "AC repair near me" put you in front of homeowners who need you today. This is the highest-return spend in a soft market. Our [local SEO playbook](/blog/local-seo-for-home-service-businesses) and [PPC guide](/blog/ppc-advertising-for-home-service-businesses) cover both.
- **Win on speed-to-lead.** When jobs are scarce, the business that answers first usually books the job. Fast call answering and instant lead response often matter more than spending more — you're just not losing the leads you already paid for.
- **Mine the customers you already have.** Your own maintenance plans, repeat-and-referral outreach and a steady review-request habit can turn your existing list into booked work. If you want help reaching past customers by email, ASP offers email campaign options in custom packages.
- **Defend your margin with retention.** In a price-sensitive market, the relationship keeps customers from shopping around on the next repair. Consistent follow-up is cheap insurance against churn.

The theme is efficiency: in a soft market you don't necessarily need a bigger budget so much as a sharper one, pointed at the people closest to buying. **Key Takeaway:** In a soft market, spend on intent and speed — GBP and high-intent ads, fast lead response, and your existing customer list — not on broad awareness. A sharper budget beats a bigger one.
